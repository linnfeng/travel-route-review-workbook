# Travel route review workbook

![Timeline Visualizer project image](https://timeline-visualizer.online/og.png)

An animated map can turn a list of places into a story, but a good route video requires more than connecting every point in an export. The maker has to decide which portion of a journey is worth showing, how the evidence should be interpreted, and whether the audience should see each location at all. This workbook offers a repeatable review process for making those decisions before a trip recap is shared.

Timeline Visualizer is a browser-based tool for turning exported Google location history into an animated travel video. Users can select a trip, adjust its route, add music and save an MP4. The [timeline visualizer](https://timeline-visualizer.online/) also offers still-image and project-file exports. I am associated with the project, and AI assisted in drafting this workbook. Examples are hypothetical; this is not an independent product test or a claim that every feature is free. Check current pricing and privacy details on the product site. Files remain in the browser, while optional detailed maps can request geographic tiles from a map provider.

## 1. Write a one-sentence brief

Before opening an editor, write down the answer to three questions: who will watch, what should they understand, and where will the video appear? A private family recap has a different level of detail from a public travel post. A presentation about an itinerary needs viewers to recognize the major stops; a personal keepsake might focus on how the journey felt rather than the most efficient route.

A sample brief could be: “Show a small group of relatives the order of cities visited on a three-day trip without revealing the hotel.” That sentence gives a practical test for later decisions. If an extra scene does not explain the order of cities, it may not be needed. If a label reveals the hotel, it fails the privacy requirement even if it looks attractive.

Set a rough duration and a single output shape before editing. A vertical clip for a phone has less room for labels than a wide presentation slide. Do not rely on an exact number of seconds as a quality measure; a shorter video that can be understood on first viewing is often more useful than a longer video with every sample included.

## 2. Inventory the source and its limits

Keep the exported file in a private folder and make a working copy. Note the source, export date, and time range, but do not include the raw data in a public repository. Location exports may contain home addresses, regular routes, or trips unrelated to the story. Limit access to people who need the file and check the export process and retention settings of the service you used.

Record what the file can and cannot tell you. A phone may have been switched off, denied a permission, or unable to obtain a reliable position. An apparent straight line between two points can be a gap rather than the path actually taken. A location sample does not prove that a person stayed at a precise address or traveled along a particular street. Note such uncertainties before making a visual sequence, because motion can make sparse data look more authoritative than it is.

Review date and time boundaries carefully. A trip across time zones can appear to start on a different local day from the one shown on a ticket. A data export may use a different convention from a calendar or camera. Where timing matters, compare the record with information you already have, such as a personal itinerary, and make a note of the convention used. Do not manufacture intermediate points simply to make a route look continuous.

## 3. Draw a disclosure boundary

Make a list of locations that may appear and a separate list of locations that must not. Examples of the latter can include a home, a private workplace, a school, or an address where someone else lives. A broad crop is not automatically anonymous: the combination of a familiar landmark, date, and starting point can still identify a person. A route can also reveal a repeated habit even when no street address is printed.

When another person is identifiable, get their permission for the specific intended audience. Permission to show a clip to family does not necessarily mean permission to publish it on an unrestricted social platform. If a person is not comfortable with the detail, choose a less precise route or leave the segment out. Consider creating a public version and a private version instead of relying on a platform's sharing setting alone.

A useful review table has four columns: segment, reason to include it, potential exposure, and action. For example, “airport to city center” may provide orientation, while a final leg to a private address may add no useful story. Record the decision as “include at city scale,” “shorten,” or “omit.” This makes the choice explicit for collaborators and reduces accidental reintroduction of an excluded point during later editing.

## 4. Build a route that can be read

Start with a small, representative segment. Inspect its beginning and ending locations and the direction of travel. Then watch the animation without reading the underlying itinerary. Can a person who did not take the trip identify the departure area, major transition and arrival? If the answer is no, improve the pacing or add a concise label instead of immediately adding more points.

Map scale changes the story. At a continental view, a city walk can disappear; at street scale, a long trip may become a rapid series of disorienting movements. Show enough geography to establish context, then move closer only when a particular stop matters. If the tool provides limited control over scale, prioritize a readable route over a dramatic camera effect.

Treat time as a design choice as well. A long transfer can be shortened, but a sequence should not imply that several distant stops happened instantly. A pause can help a viewer understand a location; a pause at every point can make a clip tedious. Use a consistent visual language so that the audience can tell whether a pause represents a significant visit or simply an editing choice.

## 5. Review visual and audio assets

At the intended playback size, inspect route contrast, labels and any text over the map. A line that is easy to see on a desktop can blend into roads on a phone. Check the opening and closing frames separately: viewers need enough context to understand the route before the marker moves, and the ending should remain visible long enough to register.

If the video contains music, use a track you have permission to share in the intended context. A license that permits private use may not permit public distribution. Record the source and any attribution requirement with the project. Listen through the exported file for abrupt starts, clipped endings or a soundtrack that overwhelms narration. A silent version can be useful for accessible viewing or later voiceover.

Avoid using images of other people or private documents as decorative material without permission. If a cover image is necessary, use an original or appropriately licensed image and distinguish a conceptual illustration from an actual screenshot. Never imply that a fictional route is a documented journey merely because it is rendered on a realistic map.

## 6. Export, compare and record the decision

Play the exported MP4 from the beginning to the end, not just the editor preview. Check for cut-off labels, unexpected pauses, audio problems and private points. Test it on a second screen size when possible. If the file is compressed for a messaging service, review the compressed copy too; small place names can become unreadable even though the original export looked clear.

Use this checklist before sending:

- [ ] The time range is the one intended for this audience.
- [ ] Start and end points do not disclose private addresses.
- [ ] The route does not claim precision beyond the source data.
- [ ] Labels and movement are readable at the actual viewing size.
- [ ] Everyone represented has agreed to this sharing context.
- [ ] Music and visual assets have appropriate permission.
- [ ] The exported file has been played through in full.

Keep a private note of the source file, the version shared, the chosen audience, and any segments deliberately omitted. Do not attach raw location exports to that public note. If a viewer later asks why a section is absent, the answer can be as simple as “it was private” or “the source was incomplete.” The point of a review is not to make a map look maximally detailed; it is to make the intended story clear without overstating the evidence or exposing unnecessary information.
